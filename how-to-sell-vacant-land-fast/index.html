How to Sell Vacant Land Fast in Florida

Vacant land is slower to sell than a house. There are fewer buyers, land loans are harder to get than home loans, and every buyer wants to know whether the lot can actually be built on. Here are your options, roughly how long each takes, and what you can do this week to speed things up.

Your options, from fastest to slowest

Sell to a direct cash land buyerWeeks

Fastest and most certain. The offer is usually below what a patient open-market sale might bring, and there is no commission.

Sell to a neighbour or adjoining ownerVaries

The person next door often values the lot most, but may not have cash ready or know how to close.

Sell to a local builderWeeks to months

Builders only buy buildable lots in areas where they are already building, and most will not chase a single out-of-state owner.

List with a land agentMonths, often longer than a house

Reaches the most buyers. You pay a commission, and many land buyers need financing that is harder to get than a home loan.

Sell it yourself onlineUnpredictable

No commission, but you field every question and handle the contract and title company yourself.

What slows a land sale down

Most land sales that drag on are not waiting for a buyer. They are waiting on paperwork. These are the usual culprits in Florida:

  • The deed is in someone else's name. If the owner of record has died, someone usually needs a court's authority to sell. Florida's shorter process, summary administration, is available when the estate's non-exempt assets are $75,000 or less or the person died more than two years ago (Fla. Stat. § 735.201).
  • Several owners on title. Everyone on the deed has to sign. Siblings who inherited a lot together need to agree before anything moves.
  • Unpaid property taxes. Unpaid Florida property taxes are sold as tax certificates, and two years later the certificate holder can apply for a tax deed and send the lot to auction (Fla. Stat. § 197.502). Back taxes do not stop a sale; they are normally paid at closing from the proceeds. Waiting is what makes them dangerous.
  • Assessments, liens and association dues. Some communities carry special assessments that stay with the land until paid, Cape Coral's utility assessments being the best-known example. Association dues and code liens work the same way. They come out of the proceeds, but they have to be known first.
  • Nobody knows what the lot is. A buyer wants the parcel ID, the size, the flood zone, whether there are wetlands and whether water, sewer and power reach it. Every unanswered question adds a round of back and forth.
  • Wetlands. The hardest problem on Florida land. Most builders will not take on wetland permitting, so a wet lot has far fewer buyers. A flood zone is a different and much softer question: it affects value, not whether a lot can sell.

Five things to do this week

  1. 1
    Find your parcel ID. Search your name or the address on the county Property Appraiser site. Our Florida Property Appraiser Directory links all 67 counties.
  2. 2
    Check the tax status. The county Tax Collector's site shows whether taxes are paid, and whether a tax certificate has been sold on the lot.
  3. 3
    Confirm who is on the deed. The deed is recorded with the county Clerk. If the owner has died, find the death certificate and any probate papers now, because the title company will ask for them.
  4. 4
    Look up the flood zone and wetlands. FEMA's Flood Map Service Center shows the flood zone. Land in Seconds, a lot-check app from the same company as VistaOne, pulls flood zone, wetlands and utilities for a Florida lot in one report.
  5. 5
    Decide what matters more: price or time. If the top possible price matters most and you can wait, listing may net more. If you want it done this month with no showings and no financing risk, a direct buyer is the faster road.

How a direct sale to VistaOne works

Send us the parcel ID and we research it from public records: the Property Appraiser record, county GIS, flood zone, wetlands, utilities and access. You get a written offer, usually within 24 to 48 hours. If you accept, a licensed Florida title company handles the title work and the money, and most purchases close in 21 to 30 days. You do not need to travel: Florida has allowed remote online notarization since 2020, so documents can be signed from wherever you live. There is no agent commission and no listing fee.

Closing costs on Florida land, in brief

Florida charges a documentary stamp tax on deeds of 70 cents per $100 of the sale price in most counties (Fla. Stat. § 201.02). It is customarily paid by the seller, but like the title insurance and recording fees, who pays what is set out in the purchase contract. Read that part of any contract before you sign.

Questions we get most often

How fast can I sell vacant land in Florida?

To a direct cash buyer, usually a few weeks once the title is clear. We typically close 21 to 30 days from a signed contract. Listed on the open market, land often takes months, and longer than a house.

Can I sell land in Florida without a realtor?

Yes. Florida does not require an agent to sell land. A title company or a real estate attorney handles the closing, the title search and the money.

Can I sell land I inherited before probate is finished?

Usually not until someone has legal authority to sell, through probate or Florida's shorter summary administration. A title company will tell you exactly what it needs before it will insure the sale.

Do I have to pay back taxes before I sell?

No. Back taxes are normally paid at closing out of the sale proceeds. Tell the buyer upfront so the numbers are right from the start.

Will a cash buyer pay full market value?

Usually not. A direct buyer prices in the time and cost of reselling or building on the lot. What you get in return is speed, certainty and no commission. If the highest price matters more than time, listing may net you more.
